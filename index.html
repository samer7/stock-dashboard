Signals are computed from real moving averages of daily closing prices: BUY when price is above MA20, MA50, and MA200 (bullish alignment). SELL when price is below MA20 and MA50 (bearish short-term trend). HOLD for mixed or transitional alignment. The reason text on each ticker shows the actual price-vs-MA percentages.

Do they work? We backtested this exact rule over ~19 years of daily data on 18 tickers. It did not beat simply buying and holding (1 of 18 tickers on price returns, 0 of 6 once dividends are counted), and its timing carried no information — the market rose after BUY days at the same rate as after any other day. What it did do is soften crashes: maximum drawdown was shallower in 16 of 18 tickers, simply because the rule keeps you out of the market about 42% of the time. So read BUY/SELL as a trend description and risk gauge ("historically reduced drawdowns, did not beat buy-and-hold") — not as trade advice. RSI and MACD were tested the same way (as trading triggers, over the same ~19 years): neither carries timing information either; they're shown as descriptions of the current trend, nothing more.

The one forecast that measured well: "expected swing" is EWMA volatility — how bumpy the next month is likely to be, not which way price goes. In our backtests it ranked calm vs. wild months with 0.59 correlation and beat naive baselines on 18 of 18 tickers; direction stayed unpredictable in every test. We also tested whether that volatility forecast could sharpen up/down odds (the textbook drift-over-vol model): it made them worse at every horizon — turbulent stretches are exactly when rebounds tend to happen. The best probability we could produce is the plain historical base rate, so that's what the report card's "odds of being higher" row shows, labeled as exactly that. Finally, we tested combining all the signals above with weights fitted by walk-forward regression: worse than the base rate on all 18 tickers at every horizon — the components carry no timing information for weights to find. That's why there is no combined "score" on this dashboard. The volatility forecast did pass one more test — sizing: holding less of a stock whenever its forecast swing ran above its own norm (and never guessing direction) cut the worst peak-to-trough drop on 18 of 18 backtested tickers and improved return-per-risk on most, at the price of a lower raw return. That measured trade-off is the "sizing by calm vs. storm" line in the report card — risk arithmetic, not advice.
